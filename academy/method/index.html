🧭 How we teach

Test, build, review: how we teach coding and chess

Every session runs in three stages. Test — we find out what your child already believes before we teach anything. Build — they make something real from a blank screen, never from a tutorial. Review — they replay the mistake until they can explain it themselves. That is the whole method. It is also why sessions are one-to-one while numbers are small.

The problem

Why most children's coding classes look like progress

Hand a child a tutorial and they will produce a working game this afternoon. The screen fills, the parent is pleased, and everyone agrees it is working. Then the tutorial is removed, and the ability goes with it. A child who has copied a game has learned to follow instructions. A child who has built one has learned to recover when it breaks — which is the only part that transfers to anything else.

The method

Three stages, in this order

1

Test — before we teach anything

We ask your child to predict what a piece of code will do, to explain a game they have just played, and to describe the move they would make next. Then we watch where they guess instead of reason. It takes about ten minutes and it is the most useful ten minutes of the term.

The reason is uncomfortable but true: a wrong belief is harder to fix than a missing one. A child who thinks a variable is a kind of button will build on that for weeks. Better to find it in the first session, while it is one sentence to correct.

2

Build — from a blank screen

Your child makes something small enough to finish in a session and deep enough to break: a character that moves, a score that changes, a rule that stops the game. No step-by-step tutorial, no copy-and-paste code, no template with the answer in it.

This is the part with the strongest foundation. Seymour Papert's constructionism holds that children build knowledge by making something shareable and then thinking about what they made — not by being told. MIT's Scratch team turned that into a learning spiral of imagine, create, play, share, reflect. The finished game is not a reward for the learning. It is the learning, and it is the thing your child carries home.

See Resnick, Sowing the Seeds for a More Creative Society and Scratch's creative learning approach.

3

Review — the mistake, out loud

When the game breaks, we stop. Your child replays what they did, finds the moment it turned, and then explains why they thought that would work. The teacher's main question is not "what is the answer" but "what did you expect to happen?"

In chess this is the same move every time: we replay the loss and find the move where the game turned. A child who can say I thought my knight was defending that square has just learned something no explanation could have installed.

What to expect

What this looks like over a term

The first two sessions look slow, and if you are watching for a screen full of colour you may wonder whether anything is happening. By week four your child has a game they built from nothing. By week eight they can tell you why it broke the first time. By the end of term they have a finished project and a written record of how they got there. The full fee structure is on the fees page.

What we don't publish. The sequence itself — the order ideas arrive in, and which ones we deliberately hold back until later — is the part that took the longest to get right. It changes what a child can do, and you will see it in the work rather than on a page.

Questions

What parents ask about the teaching

Is this just tutoring?

No. A tutor follows the school syllabus and helps with homework. We do not teach the syllabus. Sessions run in three stages: find out what the child already believes, have them build something real from a blank screen, then review the mistake until they can explain it themselves.

Why does the method need one-to-one sessions?

Because the first stage only works if the teacher can hear how one child reasons. In a room of twenty, a child who has guessed correctly sounds the same as a child who understands. One-to-one, the guessing is audible. Groups return as we add teachers.

Does my child need to be good at maths to start?

No. Children from age six start with blocks and the rules of chess. The arithmetic in a game is coordinates, counting and conditions, and it arrives because the game needs it, not as a separate lesson.

What if my child already codes?

The first session finds out what they actually understand rather than what they have copied. Many children who have followed tutorials can build a game but cannot explain why it breaks. That gap is where we start.
