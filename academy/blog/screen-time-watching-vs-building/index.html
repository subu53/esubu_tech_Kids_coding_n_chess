Screen time for kids: what your child does on the screen counts more than the hours

Hour limits treat a cartoon, a game and a coding project as the same thing. Your child knows the difference. You will too, after one ten-minute test.

Saturday, four in the afternoon. The house is quiet, which is how you know where your child is. You look in. Phone in one hand, thumb moving every few seconds, one short video after another. You stand there long enough to count. The last clip ran eleven seconds.

If this is your house, it is a normal Nairobi house. Researchers asked 563 children aged 9 to 11 in 29 Nairobi primary schools about their screen use. On school days about a third went over two hours of recreational screen time. On weekends, 74 percent did. Children in private schools reported more screen time than children in public schools.

So the quiet Saturday is common. The question is what to do about it.

The rule most parents reach for

The usual answer is a number. Two hours. One hour on school days. Phone in the kitchen by eight.

Hour limits help with sleep and with arguments, so keep yours if they work. The trouble starts when the number becomes the whole plan. An hour limit counts eleven-second clips and an hour spent building a game as the same hour. Your child spent both on a screen. Only one of them left anything behind.

The second belief is harder to let go of. Your child picks up any phone and finds the setting you were looking for. Many parents take this as a sign the child is good with technology.

Try this. Ask your child to change one thing about the app they use most. Not a setting. The way it works. Make the video stop after five minutes. Make it show a cartoon about chess instead of the next prank. They won’t know where to start, and neither would most adults. Speed with an app tells you your child has learned where to tap. It tells you nothing about whether they understand what happens after the tap.

A child fast at swiping has learned to use a product. Someone else decided how the product behaves.

What the long afternoons add up to

A Canadian team followed over 2,400 children from age 2 to age 5. Children with more screen time at 2 and 3 scored lower on a developmental screening a year or two later. Lower scores early did not predict more screen time later, which points the arrow from the screen toward development.

Read the study with care. The study used mothers’ reports, the children were toddlers, and an association falls short of proof. Your nine-year-old is not a toddler. Still, the study asks a fair question about any age: what did the screen hours replace? Talk at the table, games in the yard, boredom long enough to invent something, sleep.

Now some arithmetic for this holiday. Schools closed on 23 October and Term 1 opens on 4 January. Those are 72 days. Four hours a day on a phone across them comes to 288 hours. A full school day runs about eight hours. The same hours would fill 36 school days.

Nobody plans 288 hours. They arrive one quiet afternoon at a time.

Count what your child makes

A better test than any hour count: for each thing your child does on a screen, ask four questions.

  • Who decides what happens next, your child or the app?
  • Does the session end with something your child made?
  • When something goes wrong, does your child have to work out why?
  • Afterwards, would your child be able to explain what they did?

A short video feed fails all four. The app decides, nothing gets made, nothing goes wrong, and there is nothing to explain. Building a game in Scratch passes all four. So does a chess puzzle, a comic drawn in a paint app, a song recorded and re-recorded, a budget for the family shopping in a spreadsheet.

You don’t need to ban the feed. Give one hour a day a different job.

Tonight: sit next to your child for ten minutes of their screen time. At the end, ask “what did you make?”

Free places to start this week

Scratch is free, runs in a web browser and is designed for ages 8 to 16. Your child builds games and animations by snapping blocks together. Sit with them for the first project. Half an hour in, ask what the cat should do when it touches the edge of the screen. Watch your child go quiet and think.

Lichess is a free chess site with no adverts. The puzzle section gives a position and asks for the best move. A wrong answer is marked wrong at once, which teaches a child to look twice before moving.

Both cost data and nothing else. Use home Wi-Fi where you have it.

Check this before you believe it

Don’t take my word for any of this. The Nairobi figures come from a study published in 2018 with data gathered between 2011 and 2013, before short-video apps took over phones. The real numbers today are likely higher, and I have found no newer Nairobi study to confirm it. The Canadian study tracked toddlers. Read both, and the links sit at the bottom of this page.

Then ask the people closest to your child. What does the class teacher see on Monday mornings? What does your child say they would build if they knew how?

Your answers matter more than mine. You know which afternoons in your house go quiet.
