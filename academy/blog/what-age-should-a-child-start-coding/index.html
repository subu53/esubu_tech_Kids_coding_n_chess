What age should a child start coding? A straight answer for parents

I learned to code as an adult, the slow way. What each age is ready for, and a game in your sitting room which shows where your child stands.

I spent years working in tech before I learned to code. Not for lack of trying. I started online courses and video series and finished none of them. A folder of half-done tutorials followed me from laptop to laptop. In the end I went back to university to learn properly, as an adult with bills to pay.

I often think about how much more I’d have built if I had started at thirteen. The thought is why I teach children. It also means I’m biased, so check everything below against the sources at the end.

The answer most parents expect

Parents usually ask the question with an answer already in mind. Wait until high school. Wait until the child is good at maths. Wait until the school teaches it.

Each of these sounds careful. Each one rests on a belief worth testing: coding is hard, technical work, and a young child isn’t ready for it.

The belief mixes up two things. Typing code in a language like Python is hard for a young child, mostly because of the typing. The thinking under it, giving clear instructions in the right order, spotting a mistake, repeating a step, starts much earlier.

The Raspberry Pi Foundation, which runs the Code Club network in Kenya and around the world, puts it plainly. There is no correct age to start coding. What changes with age is the tool.

What each age is ready for

Age What fits What it looks like
4 to 5 Floor robots and instruction games, no screen needed Your child gives a toy robot, or you, steps to reach a target
6 to 7 Block coding with help, in short sessions Snapping blocks in Scratch or ScratchJr to make a character move and talk
8 to 9 Scratch projects of their own A small game with a score, a timer and a game-over screen
10 and up Typed languages, once typing is comfortable First Python programs, often after a year or two of Scratch

Scratch itself, made at MIT, is designed for ages 8 to 16. Younger children use it well with an adult nearby. The age bands are rough. Reading level and typing speed matter more than birthdays.

What waiting costs

Waiting has a cost, and it isn’t about brains. Adults learn to code. I did. The cost of waiting is time, and the confidence to look stupid.

A nine-year-old who breaks a program shrugs, tries again and thinks it’s funny when the cat walks off the screen. A thirty-year-old who breaks a program feels the clock, the job, the family, and the fear of being bad at something in public. I know which of the two quits more often. I was the second one.

Children also arrive at coding with no story about themselves yet. Nobody has told them they are “not a maths person.” Wait until fourteen and many have already decided.

Starting early has little to do with genius. It means making your first hundred mistakes while mistakes are cheap.

A five-minute test in your sitting room

You don’t need a laptop to find out if your child is ready. You need a doorway and a willingness to act like a robot.

  1. Stand in the sitting room. Tell your child you are a robot who follows instructions exactly, and nothing else.
  2. Ask them to get you to the kitchen door using only these commands: step forward, turn left, turn right.
  3. Follow every instruction exactly as given. If they say “go to the kitchen”, stand still and say “I do not understand.”
  4. When you walk into the sofa because they said one step too many, wait.

Watch what your child does next. Some laugh, stop, count the steps again and fix their instructions. Some get frustrated and want to push you there. Both are normal at six. The child who goes back and fixes the instructions is already doing what programmers do all day: debugging.

Play it three nights in a row and watch how the instructions change.

Signs your child is ready for a coding class

  • They follow and give instructions with three or four steps
  • They sit with one activity for 20 minutes when it interests them
  • They read simple words on a screen, or have an adult close by who does
  • They ask “why did it do this?” when something surprises them

Missing one of these is fine. Missing all four at five or six usually means a few more months of games like the robot one, and no rush.

Check this against your own child

The age guide comes from people who teach coding to children in many countries, but your child is one child. Read the Raspberry Pi article and the Scratch page for parents. Talk to your child’s teacher about reading and attention. Then run the robot game and trust what you see.

If your child is six or older and fixed the instructions, they are ready to make a character move on a screen. If they are fourteen and have never coded, they are ready too. They will start in a different place.
