Free coding for kids in Kenya: what's out there, and where free runs out

Everything a child needs to learn to code sits online for free. So why do so many children start, and so few finish?

I know this story from the inside. For years I worked in tech and tried to learn to code from free courses and videos. I started dozens. I finished almost none. A folder of half-done tutorials followed me from laptop to laptop, until I went back to university to learn properly, as an adult.

The lessons were never the problem. They were free, good, and everywhere. Something else was missing.

What’s free, and good

Start here before you pay anyone. Each of these costs nothing but data.

Scratch, made at MIT, lets a child build games and animations by snapping blocks together. It’s designed for ages 8 to 16 and runs on the Scratch website.

Code Clubs run free weekly coding sessions in schools and community spaces. The Raspberry Pi Foundation reports over 1,000 clubs in Kenya, in 40 of the 47 counties. Ask your child’s school if it hosts one.

The Raspberry Pi Foundation also publishes step-by-step projects, from first Scratch games up to Python.

Lichess gives free chess games and puzzles, with no adverts.

A determined child with a laptop, home Wi-Fi and one of these has everything needed to learn.

“If it’s free online, my child will learn it alone”

Some children do. Most don’t, and the reason has little to do with talent.

Free lessons give knowledge. They don’t give a plan, someone watching, or a reason to come back on the hard week. A child who meets their first stubborn bug on a Wednesday evening, alone, closes the laptop. Nobody notices. Next week there’s football. The project sits half-built, like my folder of tutorials.

Finding lessons takes five minutes. Noticing the week your child gets stuck, and quietly stops, takes a person.

What I spend my time on

Coding knowledge is free. What I sell is the structure around it. I spend hours testing each lesson on children and cutting it down until they get it, then putting the lessons in an order where each week rests on the last. Before the coding starts, we work on the maths underneath it. I call it preparing the soil.

The finished project is the point. A child who finishes one game knows they finish things. A child with ten half-games learns the opposite lesson.

Why there are no free trial classes here

Many coding schools offer a free trial class. We don’t.

A free hour shows you a child on day one, excited by something new. It doesn’t show you week three, when the project gets hard and the real learning starts. The work worth judging happens there.

So we run like school. You apply, I place your child in the right group, and they start. Fees are monthly, by M-Pesa. If you’d rather see how it goes before committing to a month, ask me on WhatsApp about paying week by week.

Make free work at home

If you go the free route, and many families should, give it the structure yourself:

  • One fixed slot, twice a week, written on the calendar
  • One project to finish before starting another
  • Sit with your child for the first ten minutes of every session
  • Ask them to show you the project every Friday
  • Join a Code Club if one runs nearby

Try Scratch with your child this weekend. Set one goal: a character which moves when you press a key.

Test it yourself

Give the free route four weeks with the structure above. Watch what happens in week three. If your child pushes through, keep going, and you’ve saved money. If the project stalls and nobody notices for a fortnight, you’ve learned what your child needs, and it isn’t more lessons.
