Walk through any Nairobi phone shop in December and watch the counters. Parents holding a child's hand. A school leaver choosing a colour. A grandmother buying a smartphone for a ten-year-old because the child asked, and because everyone in the class has one.

If you're thinking of a phone for Christmas, you're in good company. Before you buy, look at three things: the age, the kind of phone, and what you set up before your child touches it.

## "Every child in the class has one"

This is the sentence parents hear most. It comes with a second one: the school WhatsApp group for homework, and a child who misses messages without a phone.

The pressure is real. So is the cost of giving in early. A team at Sapien Labs studied over 100,000 young adults aged 18 to 24, in 163 countries. They asked when each person got their first smartphone, and measured their mind health today: mood, outlook, drive and resilience.

:::figure id="phone-study"
:::

The pattern ran in one direction. The younger the first phone, the lower the mind-health score in young adulthood. People who got a phone at 13 averaged 30 on the scale. People who got one at 5 averaged 1. Early access to social media explained about 40% of the link worldwide, and broken sleep played a part too.

Read the limits before you panic. The study is observational: it shows a pattern across a population, and it doesn't prove the phone caused anything for any one child. People recalled their childhood ages from memory. It's still one of the largest studies on the question.

:::pull
A phone at ten gives your child the world. It also gives the world your child, at ten.
:::

## Think about what the phone replaces

Picture your child's evening a year from now, with the phone. What time does the screen go dark? Who decides? Where does the phone sleep?

Most families never answer these questions out loud. The phone arrives on Christmas morning, and the rules arrive in February, after the first fight. By then the habits are set.

## Climb the ladder one step at a time

A child doesn't need a smartphone to be reachable. A kabambe makes calls, sends SMS and runs M-Pesa. For a child in primary school, it covers almost everything a parent worries about, with nothing to scroll.

:::figure id="phone-ladder"
:::

For the homework group, a family smartphone kept in the sitting room works for many households. Your child reads the messages there, in the open.

WhatsApp's own terms say a person must be at least 13 to register on their own. Use 13 as the floor for your child's own WhatsApp account, whatever the class does.

## Day-one settings, before you wrap it

If a smartphone is the gift, set it up the night before. Every step below costs nothing.

1. Create your child's Google account through Google Family Link, and link it to your phone.
2. Set a daily screen time limit and a downtime window, such as 8pm to 6am.
3. Turn on app approval, so every new app asks you first.
4. In YouTube, use a supervised experience instead of the open app.
5. In WhatsApp, go to Settings, then Privacy, then Groups, and choose "My contacts". People outside your child's contacts then have to send an invite instead of adding them.
6. Agree on where the phone sleeps. A charger in the sitting room settles it.

:::note
Write the rules on one sheet of paper, sign it with your child, and stick it on the fridge before Christmas.
:::

## Ask, then decide

Read the study summary and the Standard's report with Kenyan parents and teachers, both linked below. Then ask your child's teacher what the school expects, and whether a shared family phone covers the homework group.

Last, ask your child what they want the phone for. Calls and M-Pesa? Homework? Games? Their answer tells you which step of the ladder fits. And if part of the answer is "to make things", a phone is the wrong tool. Our post on [learning to code on a phone](/academy/blog/learn-coding-on-a-phone/) explains why.
