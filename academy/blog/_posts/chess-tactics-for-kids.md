Watch a child's first few months of chess and you'll see the same moment again and again. The game is going well. Your son moves his queen forward, proud of the attack. Two moves later a knight lands in the middle of his pieces, checks his king and takes the queen.

He knew how the knight moves. He'd been told about forks. He still didn't see it coming.

## "My child knows the rules, so more games will fix it"

Parents often think chess improves the way running does: play enough and you get better. Games help. Many children still play hundreds of games and keep losing pieces to the same few tricks.

The rules tell a child what each piece does. They don't train the eye to see the pattern in a crowded position, a second before the opponent does. Many pieces lost in children's games fall to a short list of these patterns, and each one has a name.

## What a memory test showed about strong players

In 1973, the psychologists William Chase and Herbert Simon ran a simple test. They showed a master, an advanced player and a beginner a chess position for a few seconds, then asked each to rebuild it from memory on an empty board.

On positions from real games, the stronger the player, the more pieces they placed. On pieces scattered at random, the stronger players lost most of their edge. Earlier work by Adriaan de Groot had found the same with masters.

Chase and Simon's explanation was chunks. A strong player doesn't see 25 separate pieces. They see familiar groups, such as a castled king with three pawns in front, stored from thousands of earlier games. Their memory is ordinary. Their library of patterns is huge.

:::pull
A strong player sees a fork the way you read a word: all at once, without spelling it out.
:::

So tactics are learnt the way children learn to read. They have to be seen many times, named, and found again in new places.

## The five to learn first

:::figure id="five-tactics"
:::

### 1. The fork

One piece attacks two things at once. Knights fork often, because their jump is the hardest move to see coming.

:::board fen="6k1/5ppp/2q5/3N4/8/8/5PPP/6K1 w - - 0 1" caption="White to move. The knight jumps to e7 with check and attacks the queen on c6 at the same time. The king must move, and the queen falls."
:::

### 2. The pin

A piece is stuck because moving it would expose something bigger behind it. When the piece behind is the king, the pinned piece isn't allowed to move at all.

:::board fen="4k3/5ppp/8/4n3/8/8/5PPP/4R1K1 w - - 0 1" caption="White to move. The black knight on e5 is pinned to its king by the rook on e1. White plays f2 to f4. The knight isn't allowed to move, and the pawn takes it next turn."
:::

### 3. The skewer

The reverse of a pin. The bigger piece stands in front, gets attacked, moves away, and the piece behind it is lost.

:::board fen="8/1q3ppp/2k5/8/8/8/2B2PPP/6K1 w - - 0 1" caption="White to move. The bishop goes to e4 with check. The king steps off the diagonal, and the bishop takes the queen on b7."
:::

### 4. The discovered attack

One piece moves out of the way and uncovers an attack by the piece behind it. The piece which moves is free to attack something else at the same time.

:::board fen="4kq2/5ppp/8/8/8/4B3/5PPP/4R1K1 w - - 0 1" caption="White to move. The bishop moves to c5 and attacks the queen. Moving it also uncovers the rook on e1, which now checks the king. Black has to answer the check, and the queen is lost."
:::

### 5. Back-rank mate

A king sits behind its own pawns, with no square to escape to. A rook or queen arrives on the back rank and it's checkmate.

:::board fen="3r2k1/5ppp/8/8/8/8/5PPP/6K1 b - - 0 1" caption="Black to move. Rook to d1 is checkmate. The white king's own pawns block every escape square. Teach your child to see this from both sides of the board."
:::

The last board matters most for children. Once they know the pattern, they start to see it threatening their own king, and one pawn move after castling, such as h3, gives the king a way out.

## Ten minutes a day beats one long session

Patterns stick best when practice is spread out. A 2006 review of 317 memory experiments, led by Nicholas Cepeda, found spaced practice beat the same practice crammed into one session, consistently. For your child, ten minutes a day across a week beats seventy minutes on a Saturday.

- Pick one theme on the Lichess puzzle themes page, such as forks
- Solve five puzzles from the theme
- Set the hardest one up on a real board and solve it again, saying the move out loud
- Ask your child to name the pattern before you check the answer

Rotate the theme each week. After five weeks, mix them, because in a real game nobody announces which pattern is coming.

:::

:::note
Before each move in a home game, ask your child to say "checks, captures, threats" quietly and look for each one. It feels slow for a week. Then it becomes a habit.
:::

## Test it yourself

Try the Chase and Simon test at home. Set up a position from one of your child's real games, show it for ten seconds, then ask them to rebuild it. Do it again in a month, after four weeks of puzzles. Count the pieces placed correctly both times.
