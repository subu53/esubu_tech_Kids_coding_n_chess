When the 2025 KJSEA results came out, the Ministry of Education announced a line many parents of daughters smiled at. Girls met or exceeded expectations in 10 of the 12 subjects, ahead of boys.

The gaps were wide in places. In Kiswahili, 64.86% of girls met or exceeded expectations, against 51.4% of boys. In CRE, 59.77% against 48.39%.

:::figure id="kjsea-gender"
:::

Now jump ahead a few years, to a university computer science lecture. Anthony Luvanda, a senior lecturer who writes for Brookings, reported women made up less than 9% of computer science students at his university. A Boston Consulting Group study of Africa's 4.7 million developers found women vastly underrepresented. The highest share in any African country was 24%, in Tunisia.

:::figure id="girls-gap"
:::

Ahead in most subjects at 14. A small minority in computing at 20. Something happens in between.

## "Girls aren't into computers"

Most people who say this mean it kindly. Girls like other things. Let them follow their interests.

The Grade 9 results don't show girls who struggle with hard subjects. Ability isn't the problem. Researchers call the drop-off a leaky pipeline: at every stage, a few more girls step away from computing, until few remain.

Each leak looks small and natural from inside a family. Nobody decides to steer a girl away from technology. It happens in a hundred small moments.

:::pull
Nobody tells a girl to stay away from computers. They hand the laptop to her brother first.
:::

## Look at your own house

Answer these honestly.

Who in your home sets up the new phone, or fixes the Wi-Fi when it drops? Who gets the laptop first when two children want it? When something electronic breaks, who is invited to open it and look inside?

Who does your daughter see working in technology? A woman in a family, on TV, at church? If she has never seen one, she has no picture of herself in the job.

None of these questions is about talent. All of them shape what a twelve-year-old believes she's allowed to be good at.

## What helps

- Give her the technical jobs at home: setting up devices, fixing settings, installing apps
- Share the laptop time fairly, on a written schedule
- Let her break things and fix them without rescue
- Find her a group where girls code alongside boys, or with other girls
- Look for women in tech in your network and ask one to talk to her for twenty minutes

Chess offers a quiet example. Kenya's national youth chess championships run separate girls' sections in every age group. A girl who sees other girls at the board stops asking whether she belongs there.

:::note
This week, let your daughter set up the next new device in your house, from the box to the home screen.
:::

## Check the numbers yourself

The KJSEA gender figures come from news reports of the Ministry's announcement, and different outlets rounded some numbers differently. The university figure comes from one lecturer's institution, not a national count. Read the Brookings article. It names the barriers in detail, and some will look familiar.

Then ask your daughter what she thinks people who build apps look like. Her answer tells you where the pipeline in your house stands.
