The children we teach

Most of the children who arrive here are one of four kinds.

The one who takes things apart to see how they work.

Who wants to know what is inside the thing, not just what it does. Coding gives that instinct somewhere to go.

The one who asks a question you cannot answer, then asks another.

Who is not being difficult. Who is thinking further ahead than the lesson requires, and has run out of room for it.

The one who is doing fine at school and is not stretched by it.

Fine is not the same as occupied. A child who finishes early and coasts learns that effort is optional.

The one who hates losing and has not yet learned what losing is for.

Chess teaches that faster than anything else: you lose, you look at why, you play again. They need that earlier than most.

If none of these sounds like your child, this is probably not the right programme — and it is worth knowing that before you apply rather than after.

Why this, and why now

The three things parents ask before they apply.

“Isn’t this just more screen time?”

The screens are not going away, and no argument from you is going to remove them. What changes a child’s trajectory is whether the screen is where they consume or where they make. Three evenings a week, yours is building. The rest of the week stays exactly as it is — we are not asking for the phone back.

“AI writes code now. Does this still matter?”

More, not less — but for a different reason than most programmes will give you. AI will produce the code. It will not decide what should be built, notice that an answer is subtly wrong, or explain why the program behaves as it does. Those are the skills we teach: the structure underneath, and the habit of checking. A child who has only watched AI produce things cannot tell good work from confident nonsense. A child who has built things can.

“Is this a game club with a certificate?”

A finished project. A game they can read back to you in notation. A teacher who can tell you, specifically, what your child is currently working on. If your child leaves having only had fun, we have wasted your money — and we would rather say that plainly now.

The standard

We do not fill rooms. We keep cohorts small enough that the teacher knows what every child is working on, and we hold the work at a level that requires effort from them.

Children who are used to being the quickest in the room often discover here that they are not. That is not a problem we have failed to prevent. It is the reason the room is small.

If that is uncomfortable to read, it is meant to be. It is also precisely where the growth happens — and almost nothing else in your child’s week will be asking it of them.

The fee

One fee. A small group. One teacher.

Twelve live sessions a month, capped at fifteen children, taught by the same person throughout the term. About a thousand shillings a class, for a room small enough that your child cannot hide in it.

We would rather you took a moment over this than that you took it quickly. If the fee is the only thing standing between your child and a place here, say so in the form — we will be straightforward with you about what is possible.

What the work actually is

Two disciplines. Both reward the same habit of mind.

Coding

Scratch, then Python

Children begin by assembling, not typing — but the point was never the blocks. Within a few weeks they are holding a working game of their own design, with rules they wrote.

  • Sequencing, variables, conditionals and coordinates
  • Debugging: forming a theory about why it broke, then testing it
  • One finished project per term, that they keep and can share
  • Older and ready children move on to Python
Chess

The board as a discipline

Taught properly — notation, openings with reasons, tactics, and the habit of reviewing a game you have just lost. Not a club where children are left to move pieces.

  • Algebraic notation, so they can read and record their own games
  • Openings and the ideas behind them, not lines to memorise
  • Tactics and pattern recognition, built by repetition
  • Tournament play inside the cohort, with supervised review

A note on the research. You will find programmes claiming that chess raises exam scores. The strongest reviews of that evidence do not support it — chess reliably makes children better at chess, and the transfer to grades is far smaller than the marketing says. We are not going to claim it.

What we claim is narrower, and we stand behind it: sustained attention, the habit of reviewing a game you have lost, and a child who can read a game back to you in notation.

Before you apply

The questions parents actually ask.

Is this only for exceptionally advanced children?

No. It is for children who are ready to be stretched. Readiness is not the same thing as ability — it is closer to curiosity, and to being willing to be wrong in front of other people. Some very able children are not ready, and the placement conversation is how we tell the difference.

What age should my child start?

Six is the youngest we take, because it is roughly where a child can follow a sequence of instructions and hold a rule in their head long enough to use it. There is no upper urgency — children start at nine and eleven and do very well. What matters far more than age is whether they are curious about how things work.

Why is there no free trial class?

Because we are not trying to fill a room. A free class is a sales technique, and it teaches a child that the first one does not matter. The placement conversation takes about as long, tells us considerably more, and costs you nothing but an application.

My child is six. Is that too young?

Six is where most cohorts begin. At that age it is largely Scratch and chess taught through small games rather than theory, and the work is paced for short attention spans without being condescending about it.

What if my child is not ready?

Then we will tell you, and suggest what to do instead — including coming back in six or twelve months. We would rather decline a place than take a child who will spend the term behind the room. A no from us is information, not a rejection of your child.

Will my child be out of their depth?

That is the most common worry, and the reason we do a placement conversation before a first class. Children are grouped by ability rather than age, so the seven-year-old who races ahead and the twelve-year-old who works carefully sit in different rooms. Nobody starts in a group where they cannot keep up.

Do they need any experience?

None. Children join having done no coding and never having played a proper game of chess. Others arrive already playing. The placement conversation sorts that out, which is why we do not ask you to guess now.

Will this help with school?

Indirectly, and we will not overstate it. We are not a tutoring service and we do not teach towards the KCSE. What the work does build is patience with a problem that does not yield immediately, and the habit of checking your own reasoning — which is close to what the mathematics Kenyan children most often fail actually rewards.

I have more than one child. Can they both join?

Yes — add each child in the form. They are placed on ability rather than age alone, so siblings often sit in different cohorts. Message us after applying and we will tell you what a second place costs.

What equipment do we need?

A laptop, tablet or desktop with a camera, and a connection good enough for a video call. Nothing to install — Scratch runs in the browser. Headphones help. A second screen is not necessary.

Is it safe? Do you record the classes?

The cohort is capped at fifteen and led by one known instructor. Children join from home with a parent present at the start, sessions are not recorded, nothing is published afterwards, and there is no messaging between children. If you would like to sit in on the placement conversation, please do.

Can a shy child cope?

Yes — and they are often the ones who take to chess fastest, because the board does not require them to speak up in front of the room. Coding is similar: the feedback comes from the program rather than from a teacher's opinion of them.

What if they lose interest partway through?

It usually means the cohort level is wrong rather than that the child is finished. Tell us in the first two or three weeks and we will move them to a group that fits better.

Are there chess tournaments?

We run tournaments inside the cohort, with supervised review of the games afterwards — the review is the part that teaches. When a child is ready for outside competition we will point you to the junior events running in Nairobi, and tell you honestly whether they are ready for one yet.

We are not in Nairobi.

That does not matter. Every class is live on Zoom, so we teach children anywhere with a connection. Your location only changes which cohort timetable suits you.

How do we pay, and can we start mid-term?

Payment details come on WhatsApp once a place is offered and accepted. Starting mid-term is possible when a cohort has space, and the first month is pro-rated so you are not paying for classes that already ran.
